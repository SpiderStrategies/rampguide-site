Where your package lives once it exists.

FedRAMP no longer wants your package emailed around. Agencies request access, you approve, they retrieve the current package, and every access and every denial is logged and reportable. That window is called a trust center. On the 20x track you need one from day one; on the classic track it becomes required in 2027.

RampGuide's trust center is that window, fed directly by your builds. It ships first as a stack you deploy inside your own environment, so nothing about your package leaves your boundary. A hosted version follows.

01Why you will need onethe program's own words, in plain English

The package used to be a file. Now it is a place.

FedRAMP's 2026 rules describe how a cloud provider must share its certification package with the agencies that rely on it. Four things are required, and none of them is optional on the 20x track.

  • Access on requestAn agency asks, you decide. Government customers and prospects request access; someone at your company approves or denies. Self-service signup is not allowed. Every viewer is a person you granted.
  • The current packageWhat they retrieve is the real thing. The machine-readable package and the readable documents, at the version you have published, with an inventory of what is there: name, summary, size, version, last updated.
  • A log of everythingEvery access is recorded and reportable. Who requested, who approved, who viewed what, when. The grants list doubles as your access inventory.
  • Denials reportedA refusal is an event, not silence. Denied requests are logged with a reason and reported to the program, the same way a rejected key is a log line and not a mystery in the RampGuide library service today.
02How it will worka rendering of your repo

Publish a build. That is the whole integration.

Other platforms' trust centers render their database. RampGuide's renders your git repo: the package on the trust center is the same bytes your last build produced, with dates and word counts computed from your history.

01

Build, as always

Your facts in git; one command produces the package. Nothing leaves your computer while it runs.

02

Publish the public slice

An explicit second step pushes only what agencies are allowed to see: the machine-readable package, the readable policies, the document inventory.

03

Approve viewers

Requests land in a queue. Your compliance owner approves, and the viewer receives an invitation with multi-factor sign-in. Revoke the same way.

04

Every event logged

Views, downloads, approvals, denials, with the package version each viewer saw. The change history is the quarterly report you already generate.

Two things fall out of building it this way. The inventory FedRAMP asks for already comes out of every build, word counts and last-updated dates included. And the readable documents and the machine-readable package can never disagree on the trust center, because both came out of the same build.

03Two ways to run ityour boundary first
First
Deployable · inside your environment

A stack you deploy into your own cloud boundary.

Approval workflow, encrypted storage, an access-grants table, and audit logging, packaged for your own account, including government cloud regions. Your package never leaves the boundary you already had assessed. This is the version teams with strict boundaries will ask for anyway, and it is the one we build first.

Later
Hosted · trust.rampguide.com

The same code, run by us, for everyone who would rather not.

Most teams do not want to operate an approval workflow themselves. The hosted version adds a tenant per customer to the same stack. It waits on one thing: a hosted service that holds government-facing packages needs its own authorization, and we intend to earn it the way our customers do, by generating our own package with our own engine.

04Honest statuswhat exists, what does not

Designed. Not yet built.

The design is done and the pieces it needs already exist in the product: the build emits the document inventory the program requires, the quarterly change report is computed from your history, and the library service already runs the same approve-or-deny, log-everything pattern in miniature. What does not exist yet is the stack itself, the publish command, and a price.
