01What RampGuide isin one minute, no jargon
What it is

Software plus the writing.

The engine is a free program you run on your own computer. You give it the facts about your system: which tools you use, how they are configured, who is responsible for what. It writes the whole FedRAMP package from those facts, and rewrites it whenever a fact changes.

The library is the writing, done. Professionally authored, assessor-ready answers for the tools everyone runs, kept current as FedRAMP changes. That is the part you pay for.

Who it's for

Teams selling cloud software to the government.

Cloud and SaaS companies pursuing a FedRAMP authorization, or keeping one, on either of FedRAMP's tracks: the classic Rev5 baseline or the new, faster 20x track. And the consultants and assessors who help them.

If you have a 600-page System Security Plan in Word and dread every change to it, this is for you.

What you get

Everything FedRAMP asks for, from one source.

The readable documents assessors and agencies read: your System Security Plan narratives, policies, and procedures. The data files the program now requires alongside them. And the recurring reports that keep you authorized: quarterly change reports, change notifications, vulnerability reports.

All generated from the same facts, so nothing drifts and nothing is missed.

04The paperwork problemand why it is getting worse

Selling to the government starts with a mountain of paperwork.

When one fact changes, someone hunts it down in dozens of places. They always miss some.

Before an agency can use your cloud product, FedRAMP, the government's security approval program, makes you prove you keep their data safe. The proof runs hundreds of pages in a format that is picky on purpose, and it must stay current forever. Most companies still write it by hand, in Word.

The target keeps moving. The program now wants the package machine-readable, not just printable. Rules get revised. Baselines transition. Cryptographic-module certificates lapse on a schedule nobody chose. Content that was right last quarter is quietly wrong this one.

RampGuide's answer: keep the facts in one place and generate everything else. The same facts always produce the same package, so a comparison of two builds is the complete list of what changed. No AI writes your documents and nothing leaves your computer while they are built, which is exactly what a locked-down government cloud can run.

The words on this page, in plain English
  • FedRAMPThe U.S. government's security approval program for cloud products. No approval, no federal customers.
  • ATOAuthority to Operate: the approval itself, granted after your package is assessed.
  • SSPSystem Security Plan: the big document describing how your system meets every security requirement. Usually hundreds of pages.
  • Rev5 / 20xFedRAMP's two tracks. Rev5 is the classic baseline of 323 controls; 20x is the new, faster track built on 46 Key Security Indicators.
  • Machine-readable packageThe data files (JSON) FedRAMP now requires alongside the readable documents, so the government can process submissions automatically.
  • ConMonContinuous monitoring: the quarterly reports, change notifications, and vulnerability reports you owe the program after you are authorized.
  • Assessor (3PAO)The independent third party that checks your package and your system before authorization, and every year after.
  • Trust centerThe secure website where government customers request and review your package. Required on 20x; required on the classic track from 2027.

09Coming: the trust centerproduct two · timing to be announced

Where your package lives once it exists.

FedRAMP no longer wants your package emailed around. Agencies request access, someone approves, they retrieve the current package, and every access and every denial is logged and reportable. On the 20x track this window is required from day one; on the classic track it becomes required in 2027.

RampGuide's trust center will be that window, fed directly by your builds: publish the external slice of a build and the current package, the readable policies, and the document inventory land there with the change history intact. It ships first as a stack you deploy inside your own environment, so nothing about your package leaves your boundary; a hosted version follows.
